What shooting film taught me about writing code

Thirty-six exposures a roll forces a kind of discipline that's easy to skip when storage is free. Some of it carried over.

PublishedNovember 2025
Read time5 min
CategoryPhotography

A roll of film gives you thirty-six frames. When I started shooting film after years of shooting digital, that number felt like a limitation. It took a few rolls to realize it was the whole point.

With a digital camera, the cost of a bad frame is nothing - delete it, try again, review a hundred variations on the screen until one works. With film, every press of the shutter costs something real, so you slow down before you press it. You check the light. You check the composition. You decide the shot is worth one of your thirty-six before you take it.

The same discipline shows up in code, if you let it

It's never been cheaper to write code. Generate ten variations, run them, keep whatever passes the tests, move on. That's not wrong, exactly, but it's the same trap as shooting a thousand digital frames hoping one is good: volume quietly replaces judgment.

Constraints don't just limit what you can do. They force you to decide what you actually mean before you commit to it.

The habit I brought back from photography was simple: before writing the code, write down - even just to myself - what I expect it to do and why this approach over the obvious alternative. It's the coding equivalent of checking the light before you shoot. It costs a minute and it catches a surprising number of bad ideas before they cost an afternoon.

Where the metaphor breaks down

Code isn't film - you can and should iterate on it, test it, refactor it. I'm not arguing for artificial scarcity in engineering. I'm arguing that a little deliberate friction before you start, the kind film forces on you by default, is worth building in on purpose when nothing else is going to force it.
