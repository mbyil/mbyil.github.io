The boring parts of shipping a side project

Deployment, monitoring, and the unglamorous 20% of work that decides whether a weekend project survives contact with real users.

PublishedJanuary 2026
Read time8 min
CategoryEngineering

The fun 80% of a side project is the part everyone talks about: picking the stack, sketching the data model, getting the first working version on your own machine. The other 20% is the reason most side projects never make it past that stage, and almost nobody writes about it because it isn't interesting to write about.

The list nobody enjoys

Here's what actually stood between "works on my laptop" and "a stranger can use this without asking me for help":

  • A deploy process that doesn't require me to remember six manual steps.
  • Error tracking, so I hear about a crash before a user emails me about it.
  • A backup strategy for the database, tested at least once by actually restoring it.
  • Rate limiting on anything that touches an API I'm paying for.
  • A privacy policy, even a short one, because I was asking for an email address.

None of this is technically hard. It's just tedious, and tedious work is easy to defer indefinitely when nobody's forcing the deadline. I got in the habit of treating these five items as part of "done," not as follow-up work, and it changed how many of my projects actually shipped.

The shift that helped most

A project isn't finished when it works. It's finished when it can fail safely without you standing next to it.

Once I started asking "what happens the first time this breaks while I'm not watching," a lot of the boring work stopped feeling optional. Monitoring isn't a nice-to-have feature — it's the thing that tells you whether any of the fun work you did actually holds up.

What I'd tell someone starting today

Build the fun part first, always — that's how you know if the idea is worth the rest. But budget real time for the boring 20%, and don't call it done until you've watched it fail once on purpose and recover.
